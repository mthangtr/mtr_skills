## 1. Golden Circle and Positioning Statement

Golden Circle (Simon Sinek): Why → How → What. Ask for the Why as tappable options when possible, for example:
- Simplify complex work
- Accelerate growth
- Digitally transform businesses
- Connect people and data

Positioning draft template:
> A [company type] that [what it builds] to help [customer] [core value], starting in [market] and expanding to [ambition].

Turn the Why into 3–4 **meaning territories**: clusters of words to mine. For example, Creator + simplify gives:
- Build and shape: craft, forge, mold, loom
- Clarity: clear, pure, light, lens
- Essence: essence, fold, shape

## 2. Brand Architecture

| Model | Example | Naming implication |
|---|---|---|
| Branded house | Google Drive, Google Maps | Company name must stretch across every product |
| House of brands | P&G | Company name can be quiet; products carry identity |
| Endorsed | "Product by Company" | Company name must stand alone and also read well after "by" |

If the user is undecided, recommend **Endorsed** for multi-product startups. It is flexible and can later migrate to a branded house.

## 3. Brand Archetypes (Jung, Mark and Pearson)

Offer the 4 most relevant to the domain. For tech these are usually:
- Creator: build, craft
- Sage: knowledge, precision, trust
- Magician: transformation
- Hero or Ruler: strength, leadership

## 4. Naming Spectrum

| Type | Examples | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | General Motors | Clear but restrictive; hard to trademark |
| Suggestive | Slack, Notion | Hints at meaning; often taken |
| Real word or metaphor | Apple, Linear, Stripe | Natural, but most real words are used |
| Coined | Figma, Vercel, Zalo | Ownable; needs a story; avoid a "patched" feel |
| Compound | Mailchimp, Dropbox | Self-explaining; tends to be long |

## 5. Sound Symbolism (Bouba/Kiki)

- **Soft and gentle:** l, m, n, and open vowel endings (-a, -ia, -o). Examples: Lamio, Simora.
- **Sharp and strong:** k, t, p, d, x, z, and closed endings (-ex, -ar, -on, -kt). Examples: Orvex, Kaelex.
- **Crisp and modern:** 2 syllables ending in -el, -ar or -ex. Examples: Vercel, Linear, Codex.

Pronunciation check: exactly one obvious reading in both English and the local language. Avoid "th" for Vietnamese speakers. Avoid a leading or mid-word "x", which Vietnamese reads as "s"; a final "-ex" is fine (read like Rolex).

## 6. Generation Techniques

| Technique | How | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Two-root blend | Root A + Root B, clipped cleanly | Orvex = Orbit + Vertex; Kaelex = Caelum + Vertex |
| Root + signature ending | Meaningful root + -el, -ar or -ex | Celar = Celestial + -ar; Sentar = Sentient + -ar |
| Latin morphology | Root + -fex ("one who makes") | Ordifex, Levifex (pattern of artifex, opifex) |
| Clipping | Shorten a meaningful word | Figma from figure |
| Distorted real word | Keep the sound, change the spelling | Lyft, Flickr (high saturation risk) |
| Real word, specific object | A concrete artifact or concept with a story | Palantir, Forfex (scissors), Tornus (lathe) |

**Meaning territories for "grand" briefs:** cosmos (orbit, zenith, caelum, sidus), the supernatural (numen, aeon, nous), future technology (synapse, quantum, sentient).

Grandeur works best as **grand concept, specific vessel**: Palantir is grand because of its story, not because the word itself means "grand".

## 7. SMILE and SCRATCH (Alexandra Watkins)

**SMILE** (qualities a good name has):
- Suggestive
- Memorable
- Imagery
- Legs (can extend into a brand theme)
- Emotional

**SCRATCH** (flaws to reject):
- Spelling-challenged
- Copycat
- Restrictive
- Annoying
- Tame
- Curse of knowledge
- Hard to pronounce

Give 1–2 lines per name: its main strength and its main flaw. Skip scoring tables unless the user asks.

## 8. Endorsed Test

Read each name aloud in the phrase "[Product] by [Name]", for example "Payflow by Orvex". Reject names that stumble in this phrase.

## 9. Shortlist Test

1. **Visual mockup:** wordmark, letter app icon, "Product by X", email signature, domain variants, pronunciation hint. Render it inline when a visual tool is available.
2. **Comparison:** pronunciation, spelling-by-ear, feel, story, conflict level.
3. **Tests over 3–5 days:**
   - Phone test: say the name; count how many people type it correctly.
   - Recall test: ask 5–10 people, including target customers, after 1–2 days.
   - Gut test: "Which would I announce on stage?" Best of 5 mornings.
4. If the user must decide now, favor the name that is spelled correctly after one hearing. For B2B this outweighs small differences in grandeur.

## Appendix A: Phase 1 Constraint Mapping

| Answer | Constraint |
|---|---|
| Global or regional ambition | Pronounceable in English and the local language; no diacritics; no bad meanings in target-market languages |
| Multi-product company | Not descriptive (avoid names like "XyzSoft"); must work as "Product by X" |
| B2B-heavy | Credible, stable; not cute |
| B2C-heavy | Memorable, emotional; playful is acceptable |

## Appendix B: Phase 3 Taste Calibration Set

Show 3 groups of 4 brands (multi-select). The user judges by sound and feel only, not by the company behind the name.
- Real short words: Stripe, Linear, Notion, Slack
- Coined: Vercel, Figma, Supabase, Kubernetes
- Classical or mythic: Vertex, Atlas, Oracle, Palantir

Decode the picks into a brief:
- Syllable count
- Ending sounds (-el, -ar, -ex)
- Origin type (clipped real root vs. invented)
- Shared aesthetic. For example, Stripe, Linear, Vercel and Figma are all known for product craft, which points to the Creator archetype.
- Why the rejected names lost (too casual, too long, too generic). Picking Palantir over Atlas and Oracle signals a preference for specific, story-rich names over generic ones.
